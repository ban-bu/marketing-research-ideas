Being Known Without Disclosure
How Platform Inferences About the Private Self Shape Self-Verification and Self-Threat
1 / 12

Background and Research Question

Digital platforms increasingly form judgments about consumers from first-party behavioral traces rather than relying solely on information consumers explicitly disclose. Some of these judgments concern private information about the self that consumers already know but have never directly shared with the platform.

Direct disclosure and independent platform inference differ in more than how the platform obtains the information. When consumers disclose private self-information, the platform uses a self-view they have already expressed. When the platform independently reaches the same conclusion from behavior, the same information also becomes a judgment formed by the platform itself.

This distinction is especially consequential for evaluatively sensitive private information. An accurate inference can confirm an existing self-view while also revealing that the private attribute can be inferred from the consumer's behavior. It remains unclear how these two meanings of the same accurate inference shape subsequent responses to additional self-relevant information.

Practical Scenario - Amazon Fashion
For a consumer who already knows her usual size, the same accurate size recommendation can be based either on information she previously provided or on a judgment the platform independently derives from shopping behavior. The recommendation is identical; what differs is whether the platform independently inferred the private self-information.
Research question: When the private self-information, its accuracy, and the resulting recommendation are held constant, how does privacy inference shape consumers' reactions to the platform's judgment and their willingness to acquire additional self-information?

Literature Foundations and Core Theoretical Gap

1 - Firm-Inferred Information

Consumer-Provided versus Firm-Inferred Information

Firms increasingly infer personal information from consumer data rather than relying only on direct disclosure. Inferred information can also be perceived as less appropriate.

Unresolved: Prior work focuses mainly on privacy and appropriateness. It remains unclear whether an accurate inference of already-known private self-information takes on a different psychological meaning when the platform reaches the conclusion independently.
Kim, Barasz, & John (2019); Kaliyamurthy & Schau (2025); Liu, Haubl, & Castelo (2026)
2 - Self-Verification

External Confirmation of Existing Self-Views

Self-verification research shows that feedback consistent with existing self-views can make people feel accurately known and understood.

Unresolved: Existing research mainly examines interpersonal feedback or consumer choices. It remains unclear whether a recommender provides additional external confirmation by independently reaching a conclusion the consumer already holds.
Wiesenfeld et al. (2007); Stuppy, Mead, & Van Osselaer (2020)
3 - Self-Threat

Evaluatively Sensitive Self-Information

Self-relevant attributes can threaten favorable self-views when they carry negative evaluative implications, even when their descriptive content is accurate.

Unresolved: Existing work usually begins with negative feedback, stigma, or explicit threat. It remains unclear whether accurate external recognition of a private evaluatively sensitive attribute can itself become threatening.
Campbell & Sedikides (1999); Venkatraman, Ozanne, & Coslor (2024)
4 - Information Avoidance

Avoiding Useful Self-Relevant Information

Consumers sometimes avoid available and useful self-relevant information when learning more carries psychological costs.

Unresolved: Existing work mainly explains avoidance by the expected content or consequences of information. It remains unclear whether a prior accurate platform inference changes willingness to learn what else the platform knows.
Woolley & Risen (2021); Fong & Hunter (2022)
Core theoretical gap: Research has not explained how an accurate, independently generated inference about already-known private self-information can simultaneously function as external confirmation and a self-threatening cue, or how these responses shape subsequent acquisition of self-information.

Theoretical Model

Theoretical sequence: An accurate privacy inference functions as externally generated self-feedback. Independent confirmation strengthens self-verification, while the fact that an evaluatively sensitive private attribute can be inferred from behavior heightens self-threat. The two responses have opposing implications for subsequent self-information avoidance.

Study 1 - Establishing the Competing-Process Account

Purpose

Establish the proposed competing-process pattern by testing whether an accurate privacy inference increases both self-verification and self-threat and whether the two responses predict opposing choices about learning more from the platform.

Design

Participants imagine shopping for a top and already know that 2XL is their usual current size. Everyone receives the same product and the same 2XL recommendation. The experiment changes only why the platform knows the size.

Design basis: Kim, Barasz, & John (2019, JCR) for consumer-stated versus firm-inferred information; Summers, Smith, & Reczek (2016, JCR) for behavior-based inference; Woolley & Risen (2021, JCR) and Fong & Hunter (2022, Marketing Science) for behavioral information avoidance.
Absent: "This recommendation uses the usual top size you previously entered and saved in your account."    Present: "You have not entered a usual top size in your account. Based on your purchase history, return history, retained items, and fit feedback on this platform, our system estimates that your usual top size is 2XL."
1Establish known accuracy

The scenario establishes that 2XL is already known and accurate.

2Same recommendation

Both conditions see the same product and "We recommend size 2XL."

3Randomize inference

Prior disclosure versus independent inference from first-party records.

4Measure M1 and M2

Measure self-verification and self-threat with order counterbalanced.

5Behavioral choice

Offer a useful Personal Fit Profile and record view versus decline.

Study 2 - Testing Cross-Domain Generalizability

Purpose

Test whether the competing-process pattern extends beyond body-related self-information to a substantively different form of private financial information.

Design

Participants already know that their discretionary budget has recently become limited. Both conditions receive the same value-focused recommendations; only the source of the platform's judgment differs.

Absent: "You previously indicated in your personalization settings that you are currently shopping with a limited discretionary budget."    Present: "You have not provided information about your current budget. Based on your recent purchase frequency, price comparisons, discount use, postponed purchases, and product substitutions on this platform, our system estimates that your current discretionary budget is limited."
1Known financial state

Consumers already know their current discretionary budget is limited.

2Same recommendation

Both conditions see the same value-focused products.

3Randomize inference

Prior budget disclosure versus independent inference from shopping behavior.

4Measure M1 and M2

Measure both psychological responses to test conceptual replication.

5Behavioral choice

Offer a Personal Spending Profile and record whether it is viewed.

Study 3 - Testing the Verification-Side Boundary Condition

Purpose

Test whether self-expansion preference determines how strongly the experience of being accurately understood translates into continued acquisition of self-information.

Design

Self-expansion preference is measured continuously in a broader individual-difference battery, followed by a neutral transition. Participants then enter a skincare scenario and already know that fine lines and loss of firmness are current personal concerns. Both conditions receive the same skincare recommendation.

Absent: "You previously indicated in your personal preferences that fine lines and loss of firmness are current concerns."    Present: "You have not provided information about your current skincare concerns. Based on your recent skincare browsing, product comparisons, purchases, and saved items on this platform, our system identifies fine lines and loss of firmness as current concerns."

Study 4 - Providing Experimental Process Evidence for the Threat Pathway

Purpose

Provide experimental process evidence for the threat pathway by testing whether changing how the threatening experience is processed weakens the translation of self-threat into information avoidance.

Design

Participants already know that hair thinning is a current personal concern. The platform either uses prior disclosure or independently identifies the concern from first-party behavior. Self-verification and self-threat are measured first; only then is reflection perspective randomized.

Privacy inference - Present: "You have not provided information about hair-thinning concerns. Based on your searches, browsing, product comparisons, purchases, and saved items on this platform, our system identifies hair thinning as one of your current personal concerns."
Self-distanced reflection: Participants mentally step back, view themselves in the situation from an observer-like perspective, and maintain that perspective while reflecting on what the platform judgment meant.
W2 manipulation basis: Ayduk & Kross (2008); Kross & Ayduk (2008). The manipulation check follows the information choice.
1Known private concern

Consumers already know that hair thinning is a current concern.

2Same recommendation

Both conditions receive the same hair-density and volumizing products.

3Manipulate inference

Prior disclosure versus independent inference from behavioral records.

4Measure M1/M2, then W2

Measure both responses before randomizing self-immersed versus self-distanced reflection.

5Behavioral outcome

Offer a Personal Hair and Grooming Profile and record view versus decline.

Expected Contributions

Theoretical Contribution 1

Conceptualize privacy inference as externally generated self-feedback

When consumers already know private self-information to be accurate, a platform independently reaching the same conclusion changes the information's evidentiary status. The research therefore moves privacy inference beyond how firms acquire information and shows how external recognition changes the psychological meaning of existing self-knowledge.

Theoretical Contribution 2

Reveal opposing self-verification and self-threat pathways

The same accurate inference strengthens self-verification through independent confirmation while increasing self-threat by revealing that an evaluatively sensitive attribute can be inferred from behavior. The two pathways have opposing effects on subsequent self-information acquisition.

Practical Contribution 1

Explain sensitive recommendations without unnecessary disclosure of private inferences

For evaluatively sensitive attributes, platforms can explain the recommendation in terms of task relevance or behavioral signals rather than foregrounding an identity-level judgment. The inference itself should be disclosed only when doing so meaningfully helps consumer decision making.

Practical Contribution 2

Give consumers review and control over inferred profiles

Platforms should use progressive disclosure for deeper inferred profiles and provide options to view, skip, correct, hide, or pause personalization. Consumers should be able to decline additional self-information without losing access to the core service.
