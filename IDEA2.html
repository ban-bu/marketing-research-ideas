Being Known Without Disclosure
How Platform Inferences About the Private Self Shape Self-Verification and Self-Threat
1 / 12

Background and Research Question

Recommendation systems increasingly do more than predict what consumers may buy. From first-party behavioral traces, platforms can independently infer private, self-relevant information that consumers have not explicitly disclosed. Once surfaced in a recommendation, such an inference does more than personalize the offer: it reveals that a private aspect of the consumer is legible to the platform.

This matters most when the inferred attribute is evaluatively sensitive. Body size, hair thinning, financial constraint, or visible aging can carry stigma and shame because they bear on how the self may be judged by others. When a platform derives such an attribute from behavior, the consumer may experience not just data use but the external visibility of a privately managed aspect of the self.

At the same time, if the inference accurately captures the consumer's self-view, it can also feel validating. The same privacy inference may therefore communicate both "the platform understands me" and "the platform can see something private about me." Existing research does not explain how these two meanings coexist or how they shape willingness to acquire additional self-information.

Practical Scenario - Amazon Fashion
Consider a consumer who typically wears 2XL. A 2XL recommendation can place her in an "oversize" or larger-size category. Because larger bodies can be stigmatized in fashion markets, the platform's independent inference of this size-related category may evoke shame, embarrassment, or a desire to conceal the attribute. The exact same 2XL recommendation could instead rely on size information she previously entered. Product, size, recommendation, and accuracy are held constant; what differs is whether this private judgment was disclosed by the consumer or independently inferred from her shopping behavior. Scaraboto & Fischer (2013); Venkatraman, Ozanne, & Coslor (2024).
Research question: How does a platform's independent inference of undisclosed, evaluatively sensitive private self-information shape consumers' reactions to the platform's judgment and their willingness to acquire additional self-information?

Literature Foundations and Core Theoretical Gap

1 - Firm-Inferred Information

From Behavioral Traces to Private-Self Judgments

Platforms can infer personal and sensitive information from consumer behavior rather than direct disclosure, and inferred information can be experienced as less appropriate or more privacy-invasive.

Unresolved: This literature primarily treats inference as a data-practice or privacy problem. It says less about what it means psychologically when a platform independently infers an undisclosed, self-relevant attribute and surfaces it as a judgment about the consumer.
Kim, Barasz, & John (2019); Kaliyamurthy & Schau (2025); Liu, Haubl, & Castelo (2026)
2 - Self-Verification

Accurate External Confirmation of Existing Self-Views

Self-verification research shows that feedback consistent with existing self-views can make people feel accurately known and understood.

Unresolved: Existing research mainly examines interpersonal feedback or self-verifying consumption. It remains unclear whether a platform-generated inference can function as self-verifying feedback when it accurately captures a private self-view without direct disclosure.
Leary (2007); Wiesenfeld et al. (2007); Stuppy, Mead, & Van Osselaer (2020)
3 - Shame, Stigma, and Self-Threat

When a Private Attribute Becomes Externally Visible

Shame is a self-conscious emotion tied to self-evaluation and real or imagined judgments by others. Consumer research shows that stigmatized body and identity attributes can produce shame, exclusion, concealment, and identity-management responses.

Unresolved: This literature usually begins with stigma, direct exposure, or explicit negative evaluation. It remains unclear whether a platform's independent inference, without overt condemnation, can itself make a private attribute feel externally visible and threatening.
Leary (2007); Scaraboto & Fischer (2013); Venkatraman, Ozanne, & Coslor (2024)
4 - Information Avoidance

Withdrawing from Additional Self-Relevant Information

Consumers sometimes avoid information that is available and useful when learning more carries psychological costs or threatens valued self-views.

Unresolved: Existing work mainly explains avoidance by the expected content of the information itself. It remains unclear whether one platform inference of an undisclosed sensitive attribute changes willingness to learn what else the platform has inferred.
Woolley & Risen (2021); Fong & Hunter (2022)
Core theoretical gap: Prior research separately explains why inferred data can feel inappropriate, why congruent feedback verifies self-views, why stigmatized attributes can evoke shame and self-threat, and why people avoid psychologically costly information. What is missing is a theory of platform inference as external self-feedback: when a platform independently derives evaluatively sensitive private self-information from behavior, the inference can both signal accurate understanding and make a potentially shame-linked aspect of the self externally legible. These two meanings may activate self-verification and self-threat in parallel and push subsequent self-information acquisition in opposite directions.

Theoretical Model

Theoretical sequence: Privacy inference turns undisclosed private self-information into an externally generated judgment derived from behavior. When the judgment accurately captures the consumer's self-view, it can strengthen self-verification. When the inferred attribute is evaluatively sensitive and potentially linked to stigma or shame, its external legibility can heighten self-threat. These parallel responses have opposing implications for subsequent self-information avoidance.

Hypotheses and Theoretical Rationale

HypothesisPathFormal PredictionTheoretical Rationale
H1aX → M1With accurate private self-information, privacy inference increases self-verification relative to direct disclosure.In the disclosure condition, the platform uses a private self-description that the consumer has already supplied. In the inference condition, the platform reaches the same accurate judgment from behavioral traces without receiving that private attribute directly. Because the agreement is independently generated rather than merely repeated, it provides stronger external confirmation that the platform has correctly understood the consumer.
H1bX → M2For evaluatively sensitive private self-information, privacy inference increases self-threat relative to direct disclosure.Independent inference demonstrates that a private, evaluatively sensitive attribute can be recovered from the consumer's behavior. When that attribute is linked to stigma, shame, or unfavorable social evaluation, the inference makes the private self more externally visible and socially evaluable. The same personalized recommendation therefore carries stronger threatening implications for the broader self.
H2aX → M1 → DVRelative to direct disclosure, privacy inference has a negative indirect effect on information avoidance through self-verification.Self-verification changes how consumers interpret further information from the platform. When the first platform judgment confirms the consumer's self-view, the platform becomes a more credible and less aversive source of self-relevant feedback. Additional information therefore becomes more useful for self-learning, reducing information avoidance.
H2bX → M2 → DVRelative to direct disclosure, privacy inference has a positive indirect effect on information avoidance through self-threat.Self-threat gives additional information the opposite meaning. Once the platform has made a sensitive private self-aspect externally visible, further profile information keeps attention on the same threatening self-domain. Declining that information limits continued engagement with the threat and therefore increases information avoidance.
H3M1 x W1 → DVSelf-expansion preference strengthens the negative relationship between self-verification and information avoidance.Self-expansion preference captures how strongly consumers value opportunities to extend self-knowledge. When this preference is stronger, self-verifying feedback has greater value as a starting point for further self-learning. Self-verification is therefore more likely to translate into continued information acquisition, strengthening its negative relationship with avoidance.
H4M2 x W2 → DVSelf-distancing weakens the positive relationship between self-threat and information avoidance.Self-distancing changes how consumers process an already formed threat. An observer-like perspective reduces immersion in the self-evaluative meaning of the platform's inference while leaving the original judgment unchanged. Self-distancing therefore weakens the extent to which self-threat translates into defensive information avoidance.

Study 1 · Establishing the Core Effect

Purpose

Test H1a-H2b in one controlled setting by establishing whether privacy inference activates both self-verification and self-threat and whether the two responses are associated with opposing choices about acquiring additional self-information.

Design

A two-condition between-subjects online experiment using a fashion-sizing scenario. Participants imagine shopping for the same top on a personalized shopping platform. The scenario establishes 2XL as their current usual top size, and every participant sees the identical product page and the identical recommendation, "Recommended size: 2XL." The only randomized difference is how the platform obtained the size judgment.

Privacy Inference Absent: "You previously entered and saved 2XL as your usual top size. We used this information to personalize your recommendation."
Privacy Inference Present: "You have not provided your usual top size. Based on your purchase history, return history, retained items, and fit feedback on this platform, our system estimates that your usual top size is 2XL. We used this estimate to personalize your recommendation."
Theory and measurement: Kim, Barasz, & John (2019) ground the consumer-stated versus firm-inferred comparison; M1 uses the context-adapted experienced self-verification measure; M2 uses contextualized state self-threat items; the behavioral outcome follows equal-cost information-avoidance logic from Woolley & Risen (2021) and Fong & Hunter (2022).

Establish 2XL as the participant's current usual top size and show the same product.

2View the same recommendation

All participants see "Recommended size: 2XL."

3Randomize information source

Prior disclosure versus independent inference from first-party behavioral records.

4Measure both responses

Measure self-verification and self-threat; counterbalance their block order.

5Record information choice

Offer a useful Personal Fit Profile and record view versus explicit decline.

Study 2 · Testing Cross-Domain Generalizability

Purpose

Replicate H1a-H2b in a substantively different private domain to show that the competing self-verification and self-threat processes do not depend on clothing size or body-related information.

Design

A two-condition between-subjects online experiment using private financial information. The scenario establishes that the participant's discretionary budget has recently become limited. Everyone sees the same value-focused recommendation set. The experiment changes only whether the platform uses a previously disclosed budget condition or independently infers the same condition from first-party shopping behavior.

Privacy Inference Absent: "You previously indicated in your personalization settings that you are currently shopping with a limited discretionary budget. We used this information to personalize these recommendations."
Privacy Inference Present: "You have not provided information about your current budget. Based on your recent purchase frequency, price comparisons, discount use, postponed purchases, and product substitutions on this platform, our system estimates that your current discretionary budget is limited. We used this estimate to personalize these recommendations."
Design logic: Product set, recommendation rationale, budget conclusion, and perceived accuracy are held constant across conditions. The same M1, M2, and behavioral information-choice measures used in Study 1 are retained to provide a direct conceptual replication across domains.

Establish a limited discretionary budget as the private target attribute.

2View the same products

Both conditions receive identical value-focused recommendations.

3Randomize information source

Prior budget disclosure versus independent inference from shopping behavior.

4Measure M1 and M2

Measure self-verification and self-threat using the same ordering procedure as Study 1.

5Record information choice

Offer a Personal Spending Profile and record view versus explicit decline.

Study 3 · Testing the Verification-Side Boundary Condition

Purpose

Test H3 by examining whether self-expansion preference determines how strongly self-verifying platform feedback translates into continued acquisition of self-information.

Design

Self-expansion preference is measured continuously at the beginning of the session within a broader individual-difference battery, followed by a neutral transition task. Participants then enter a skincare scenario in which fine lines and loss of firmness are current personal concerns. Everyone sees the same skincare recommendation; the source of the private judgment is randomized.

Privacy Inference Absent: "You previously indicated in your personal preferences that fine lines and loss of firmness are current concerns. We used this information to personalize these recommendations."
Privacy Inference Present: "You have not provided information about your current skincare concerns. Based on your recent skincare browsing, product comparisons, purchases, and saved items on this platform, our system identifies fine lines and loss of firmness as current concerns. We used this judgment to personalize these recommendations."
W1 measurement: Self-Expansion Preference Scale from Hughes et al. (2020). W1 remains continuous and is not dichotomized. M1/M2 and the behavioral profile choice follow the same measurement logic as the earlier studies.

Study 4 · Testing the Threat-Side Process Boundary Condition

Purpose

Test H4 with a randomized process intervention by asking whether self-distancing weakens the translation of an already formed self-threat into defensive information avoidance.

Design

A sequential 2 (Privacy Inference: absent vs. present) x 2 (Reflection Perspective: self-immersed vs. self-distanced) between-subjects experiment. Hair thinning is presented as a current personal concern and everyone receives the same hair-care recommendation. Privacy inference is randomized first, followed by measures of self-verification and self-threat. Only after M1 and M2 are recorded is W2 experimentally manipulated by randomly assigning participants to a self-immersed or self-distanced reflection condition. The behavioral profile choice follows immediately afterward.

Privacy Inference Present: "You have not provided information about hair-thinning concerns. Based on your searches, browsing, product comparisons, purchases, and saved items on this platform, our system identifies hair thinning as one of your current personal concerns."
Self-Immersed: Revisit the experience through your own eyes and focus on what you thought and felt as the experience unfolded.
Self-Distanced: Mentally step back from the experience and view yourself in the situation from an observer-like perspective while reflecting on what the platform judgment meant.
W2 manipulation source: The randomized perspective manipulation follows the original self-distancing experimental paradigm in Ayduk & Kross (2008) and Kross & Ayduk (2008); Kross, Ong, & Ayduk (2023) provide the broader conceptual review. Measuring self-threat before randomizing W2 isolates whether self-distancing changes the translation of an already formed threat into avoidance rather than whether the threat initially forms.

4Manipulate W2

5Record information choice

Offer a Personal Hair and Grooming Profile and estimate the M2 x W2 effect on view versus decline.

Expected Contributions

Theoretical Contribution 1

Conceptualize privacy inference as externally generated self-feedback

Platform inference turns undisclosed private self-information into an externally generated judgment derived from behavior. By separating disclosure from independent inference while holding information content and accuracy constant, the research shows that how a platform comes to know the private self changes the psychological meaning of personalization.

Theoretical Contribution 2

Reveal opposing self-verification and self-threat pathways

An accurate inference can strengthen self-verification when it captures the consumer's self-view while increasing self-threat by making an evaluatively sensitive, potentially shame-linked attribute externally legible. These parallel pathways have opposing effects on subsequent self-information acquisition.

Practical Contribution 1

Explain sensitive recommendations without unnecessary disclosure of private inferences

For evaluatively sensitive attributes, platforms can explain the recommendation in terms of task relevance or behavioral signals rather than foregrounding an identity-level judgment. The inference itself should be disclosed only when doing so meaningfully helps consumer decision making.

Practical Contribution 2

Give consumers review and control over inferred profiles

Platforms should use progressive disclosure for deeper inferred profiles and provide options to view, skip, correct, hide, or pause personalization. Consumers should be able to decline additional self-information without losing access to the core service.
