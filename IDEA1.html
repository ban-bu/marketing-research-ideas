Who Is Responsible for What We Create with AI?

How Input Representation Shapes Shared Agency and Accountability
1 / 12

Background and Research Question

Generative AI has lowered barriers to expressing intent in human–AI interaction by allowing users to describe desired outcomes directly in natural language. As generative tasks become more complex, input practices increasingly range from free-form natural-language descriptions to structured formats that are easier for AI systems to parse and execute. In AI video generation, for example, the same creative intention can be expressed either as a continuous paragraph or as structured input organized shot by shot.

The two formats differ in more than surface presentation. Natural-language input leaves more interpretation of user intent, task decomposition, and generative planning to the AI. Machine-oriented structured input requires users to perform part of that decomposition and planning in advance, so users contribute not only what they want but also part of the structure used to translate that intention into generation steps.

When users contribute more to generative planning while the AI remains responsible for interpretation, generation, and integration, the contribution structure underlying the same final output also changes. This raises a broader question: can subtle differences in how users participate in human–AI creation shape how responsibility is understood when the resulting work later receives a negative outcome?

Practical Scenario: Google Gemini
In Google Gemini, a consumer can describe a short promotional video in a natural-language prompt. The same creative information can also be presented as structured input organized shot by shot. In both cases, Gemini interprets the request and generates the content. Structured input, however, requires the consumer to complete more of the task decomposition before generation begins. Even when the final AI output is the same, the consumer participates differently in the process that produces it.Google Gemini, 2026

Research question: When users and generative AI jointly create an output, how does the way users participate in its creation shape who is held responsible when that output later receives a negative outcome?

Literature Foundations and Core Theoretical Gaps

1: INPUT REPRESENTATION

Input Representation in Human–AI Creative Work

Recent generative AI research shows that representation can shape human–AI creative work. Different formats can change how creative tasks are organized and how much effort users expend. Gordetzki et al. (2026) further show that textual and visual representations produce different human–AI agency configurations.

Unresolved: Existing research focuses mainly on representations generated by AI or presented to users. Less is known about representations at the user-input stage. It remains unclear whether expressing the same creative intention in natural language rather than in a machine-oriented structured form changes consumers’ perceived shared agency with the AI.

Cillo & Rubera (2025), Gordetzki et al. (2026)

2: PERCEIVED SHARED AGENCY

Perceived Shared Agency

Perceived shared agency captures the experience that an action and its outcome are jointly produced with another actor. Recent research has extended this construct to customer–AI interactions and finds that stronger shared agency is associated with greater willingness to use and purchase AI-powered services.

Unresolved: Existing research establishes shared agency as an important experience in human–AI interaction, but its consequences after a negative outcome are less understood. Whether perceived shared agency formed before the negative outcome shapes how much responsibility consumers subsequently assign to the AI has not been directly established.

Bolt et al. (2016), Loehr (2018), Voyer et al. (2025)

3: AI FAILURE AND RESPONSIBILITY

Responsibility Attribution After AI Failure

Research on AI failure shows that responsibility judgments depend on both failure characteristics and the broader interaction context. Zhang et al. (2026) examine failure locus, controllability, and intentionality, while Castillo et al. (2026) show that interaction conditions, user expectations, failure type, and failure severity can influence responsibility attribution.

Unresolved: These studies identify several antecedents of responsibility, including factors that arise before failure. They do not, however, directly test perceived shared agency as a psychological antecedent of responsibility attribution. Whether shared agency formed during human–AI interaction shapes subsequent responsibility attributed to the AI after failure therefore remains unclear.

Zhang, Wu, & Liu (2026), Castillo, Canhoto, & Said (2026)

4: HUMAN–AI COLLABORATION AND RESPONSIBILITY

Human–AI Collaboration and Responsibility

Related research already shows that the collaborative context can change responsibility allocation. Cooperative versus competitive task structures alter how responsibility is allocated between humans and robots, responsibility can be shared between human and AI actors in joint decision-making, and AI customization can increase users’ sense of personal responsibility for negative outcomes.

Unresolved: These studies examine relatively visible differences in task structure, shared decision-making context, or AI customization. Input representation is a subtler distinction: the formal roles of the user and the AI need not change, while the same creative intention is simply organized differently before submission. Whether this difference changes later responsibility judgments, and whether perceived shared agency explains the effect, remains unclear.

Lei & Rau (2023), Brailsford et al. (2025), Yu et al. (2026)

Core theoretical gaps: Gap 1. From input representation to shared agency. Research has not established whether different user-side representations of the same creative intention produce different levels of perceived shared agency with the AI.
Gap 2. From shared agency to responsibility. Although prior research connects human–AI collaboration with responsibility attribution, it has not established whether perceived shared agency itself subsequently shapes how much responsibility consumers assign to the AI after a negative outcome.
Together, these gaps leave unclear whether a more subtle difference in how users represent the same creative intention can alter post-failure responsibility attributed to the AI, and whether perceived shared agency provides the psychological link.

Theoretical Model

Theoretical sequence: Input representation redistributes contributions between consumers and AI within the generative process. Machine-oriented structured input requires consumers to perform part of the task decomposition and generative planning, while the final output still depends on the AI’s interpretation, generation, and integration. This creates a more interdependent configuration of contributions, increasing perceived shared agency. Perceived shared agency then creates a pre-outcome representation that the outcome was produced through contributions from both the consumer and the AI. This production-process representation carries forward into subsequent responsibility judgments, reducing the relative share of responsibility assigned to the AI and thereby increasing decisional forgiveness.
Top-Journal Structural Benchmark
Kang, Kim, & Lakshmanan (2025), Journal of Marketing, 89(1), 56–76

Input representation refers to the form in which the same underlying information or intention is organized and expressed when provided to an AI system.
The study compares natural-language input, which conveys a creative intention in free-form natural language, with machine-oriented structured input, which decomposes and organizes the same intention into units that more closely correspond to the generation process.

M1Perceived Shared Agency
Perceived shared agency is the subjective experience that an action and its outcome are produced jointly with another actor.
Applied to consumer–AI interaction, it captures the extent to which consumers experience themselves and the AI as joint contributors to the formation of an outcome.
Voyer et al. (2025)The Perceived Shared Agency scale developed and validated by Voyer et al. (2025) for customer–AI interactions, with only the referent changes necessary for the generative-AI task used in the study.
M2Perceived AI Responsibility
Perceived AI responsibility refers to the extent to which an AI system is judged to bear responsibility for an outcome.
For an outcome jointly shaped by the consumer and the AI, it captures how much responsibility for a subsequent negative outcome the consumer assigns to the AI.
Brailsford et al. (2025); Castillo et al. (2026)Primary measure: responsibility allocation adapted from Pham et al. (2010). Participants distribute a fixed total amount of responsibility across relevant contributors; the share assigned to the AI serves as the primary measure.
DVDecisional Forgiveness
Decisional forgiveness concerns a person’s decision to reduce avoidance or retaliatory responses after a transgression and to adopt a less destructive behavioral stance toward the responsible party.
Following a negative outcome in AI-assisted work, it reflects the consumer’s willingness to remain open to future interaction with the AI rather than withdraw from or terminate the relationship, even when negative feelings may persist.
Xie et al. (2025); Worthington et al. (2007)Three-item decisional forgiveness measure used by Tsarenko & Tojib (2012), adapted to the AI used in the study; items capture intentions to continue the relationship, remain friendly in future interactions, and avoid terminating the relationship.
W1Coordination Cue
A coordination cue is a visible interface signal that makes the handoff of work between actors explicit.
Within a consumer–AI workflow, such a cue makes the link between the consumer’s contribution and the AI’s subsequent work visible, clarifying how one contribution is carried forward into the next stage of the task.
Le et al. (2025)Adapted from Le et al. (2025) task-transfer logic: the cue-present condition explicitly shows that the user’s input has been transferred to the AI generation stage; the cue-absent condition shows only generic processing information.
W2Attributional Complexity
Attributional complexity is an individual tendency to prefer multifaceted rather than simple causal explanations when interpreting behavior and outcomes.
When consumers interpret a negative outcome involving AI, greater attributional complexity makes them more inclined to consider multiple contributing causes, relationships among those causes, and situational influences rather than relying on a single-cause explanation.
Fletcher et al. (1986) (foundational construct and scale)Original 28-item Attributional Complexity Scale (ACS) developed by Fletcher et al. (1986), assessing the tendency to consider multiple and interacting causes when explaining behavior and outcomes.

H1X → M1Machine-oriented structured input increases perceived shared agency relative to natural-language input.Natural-language input primarily specifies the desired outcome, leaving more task decomposition and organization to the AI. Machine-oriented structured input shifts part of this task structuring to the consumer while the AI continues to interpret, generate, and integrate the content. This more interdependent contribution structure makes the output more likely to be experienced as jointly produced.
H2X × W1 → M1Coordination cues strengthen the positive effect of machine-oriented structured input on perceived shared agency.Structured input gives consumers a greater role in task decomposition and information organization before AI generation, but the connection between this contribution and the AI’s subsequent generation may remain implicit. A coordination cue makes this handoff explicit by showing that the consumer’s input has entered the AI generation stage. The cue therefore does not create interdependence; it makes the interdependence created by structured input easier to perceive. As a result, structured input is more likely to be experienced as joint production, strengthening its positive effect on perceived shared agency.
H3M1 → M2Greater perceived shared agency reduces the relative share of responsibility assigned to the AI for a negative joint outcome.Perceived shared agency creates a pre-outcome representation that the outcome was produced through contributions from both the consumer and the AI. This understanding of how the outcome was produced carries forward into subsequent responsibility judgments. As shared agency increases, concentrating responsibility on the AI becomes less consistent with the consumer’s prior representation of the production process, thereby reducing the relative share of responsibility assigned to the AI.
H4M2 → DVGreater perceived AI responsibility decreases decisional forgiveness.Assigning more responsibility for the negative outcome to the AI provides a stronger basis for avoidance and punitive responses toward the AI. Greater perceived AI responsibility should therefore reduce consumers’ willingness to maintain a less destructive and more open stance toward future interaction.
H5M1 × W2 → M2Attributional complexity strengthens the negative relationship between perceived shared agency and perceived AI responsibility.Perceived shared agency provides multi-actor causal information about how the output was produced. Consumers higher in attributional complexity are more likely to integrate multiple and interacting causes when interpreting the rejected outcome, and therefore to use shared-agency information in allocating responsibility. The same increase in perceived shared agency should consequently produce a larger reduction in the responsibility assigned to the AI.

Study 1 · Establishing the Core Effect

Purpose

Test H1 by examining whether different representations of the same creative information produce different levels of perceived shared agency.

Design

A two-condition between-subjects experiment using an approximately 10-second promotional-video task. All participants receive the same fixed creative brief and the same required information for the video. In the natural-language condition, participants express the brief as one coherent prompt. In the structured-input condition, they organize the same information as structured input organized shot by shot. Both groups then receive the same standardized AI-generated video draft and complete the Perceived Shared Agency measure before any negative outcome is introduced.

Theory and measurement: Gordetzki et al. (2026, ISR) provide theoretical grounding for representation and human–AI agency configurations; Voyer et al. (2025) provide the Perceived Shared Agency measure for customer–AI interactions.

Study 2 · Testing the Downstream Accountability Process

Purpose

Replicate H1 and test the two downstream accountability relationships: whether greater perceived shared agency reduces the relative share of responsibility assigned to the AI (H3), and whether greater perceived AI responsibility reduces decisional forgiveness (H4).

Design

A two-condition between-subjects experiment using a five-slide promotional-presentation task. All participants receive the same fixed presentation brief and required information. In the natural-language condition, the requirements are expressed as one continuous brief; in the structured-input condition, the same requirements are organized slide by slide. After participants view the same initial AI-generated presentation draft, Perceived Shared Agency is measured before review. Both groups then receive the same final five-slide presentation, submit it for supervisor review, and are informed that it did not pass the review and was therefore rejected.

Design benchmarks: Goenka & Bagchi (2025, JCR, Study 2) and Bharti & Sussman (2025, JCR, Experiment 6) provide multi-stage process-design benchmarks; the responsibility-allocation approach follows Pham et al. (2010); the rejection scenario draws on Yue & Li (2023, Frontiers in Psychology, Experiment 2), which uses a ChatGPT collaboration task.

Expected Contributions

Theoretical Contribution 1

Linking Shared Agency During Production to Responsibility After Negative Joint Outcomes

Prior research has examined shared agency and responsibility attribution in human–AI interaction largely as separate questions. This research connects them by showing how user-side input representation shapes perceived shared agency before a negative outcome is known, which then influences the relative share of responsibility assigned to the AI after jointly produced work is rejected. Coordination cues and attributional complexity further distinguish the formation of shared agency from its later use in responsibility judgments.

Theoretical Contribution 2

Reframing Input Representation as a Process Design Feature of Human–AI Co-Creation

Natural-language and machine-oriented structured input differ not only in format, but also in how task decomposition, information organization, and generative planning are distributed between users and AI. By holding creative information and AI output constant, this research shows that information organization itself can change how users understand their own role in jointly producing an outcome with AI.

Managerial Contribution 1

Platform Design: Shape Co-Creation Through Input Structure and Handoff Cues

When platforms want users to experience themselves as co-creators, they can use structured input templates and explicitly show that user input has been transferred to the AI generation stage. When the task is closer to one-way delegation, platforms can reduce requirements for structured decomposition and explicit handoff cues. Input design is therefore not only an efficiency tool, but also a lever for shaping shared-agency experiences.

Managerial Contribution 2

Platform Governance: Preserve Contribution Records for Accountability and Revision

When jointly produced work is rejected, platforms need not rely solely on a generic rejection message. They can preserve the user's original input, structured requirements, and records showing how those inputs entered the AI generation process, then allow users to review and revise them before regeneration. This gives users a clearer basis for responsibility judgments and allows them to base decisions about revision and continued use on a traceable contribution history.
